Interest

The intersection of technology and private markets.

When I got to UT Austin in 2024 and talked to others at McCombs, the only two paths everyone went down in the finance space were management consulting and investment banking. I was quite set on working in private equity, as I had already been introduced to the space. When I was 16, I worked at ZT Corporate, a PE firm in Houston, where I became very curious about the private markets.

I joined a couple of orgs on campus that were meant to prepare us for recruiting. However, as I got deeper into it, I lost interest. We would have weekly meetings where we learned our technicals: financial statements, accounting, a bit about M&A, and leveraged buyouts. All of those concepts were fun to learn, but everything felt more like memorizing and rehearsing than truly learning. Everyone was preparing for the same interviews, giving the same answers and competing for the same handful of seats, and almost nobody stopped to ask what any of it was actually for.

What held my attention was the businesses themselves. I liked reading about a company and working out why it made money, why an owner had spent thirty years building it, and what would have to be true for it to be worth buying. That part never felt like studying. The technicals were a vocabulary for talking about a business, not the thing itself, and I think that distinction is why the memorizing bothered me so much.

So I started building instead. Building something is the fastest way I have found to actually learn it, because you cannot memorize your way through it. Either the thing works or it does not, and figuring out why it does not is where the understanding actually happens. A pitch can be rehearsed. A system that has to run tomorrow morning cannot be.

The two interests stopped feeling separate once I started pointing software at work I had already done myself. Sourcing deals meant finding businesses, researching owners, tracking down contact information and following up — necessary, repetitive, and almost entirely undifferentiated. It was also the work nobody I knew actually wanted to do. That gap is what I find genuinely interesting: not finance on its own and not software on its own, but using one to remove the parts of the other that never needed a person in the first place. That is what I am building at Equitle, and the longer version of the argument is in Autonomous Future.

Autonomous Future

Shariq Hafizi · September 2026

People will make a larger impact on the world when their energy is shifted toward solving bigger problems that help humanity. Spending my youth between Austin and San Francisco, I’ve seen the world change faster than I ever expected. Not too long ago, the idea of getting into a car with nobody in the driver’s seat seemed crazy. Today, I can have a self-driving car drop me off at the airport and barely think twice about it. It isn’t surprising or exciting anymore. It just feels normal. Today, drivers are beginning to get replaced and over the next ten years, many more jobs will follow. I don’t necessarily see this as a bad thing. I think we are moving toward a world where humans spend less of their lives doing mundane work and more of their energy working on problems they actually care about.

There is a lot of change happening in physical AI, but I am much more interested in what happens to white collar work. Some of the smartest people I know still spend ten or twelve hours a day behind a computer operating software. An investment banking analyst is glued to a monitor building pitch decks and updating financial models. A private equity analyst searches databases, builds lists of companies, finds contact information and sends emails to business owners. These people may enjoy finance or investing, but very few of them are passionate about formatting slides or updating spreadsheets. They do it because it is part of the job, because it pays well and because they hope doing it will eventually allow them to work on more interesting problems.

Talking to my friends, I’m already noticing a cultural shift. More people around me want to start companies, join smaller teams or work on ideas that they find genuinely interesting. I think AI will accelerate this shift. As more of the basic work in entry-level jobs becomes automated, companies will naturally need fewer people simply executing tasks. The value of a person will increasingly come from their ability to think creatively, make decisions, understand other people and care deeply about the problem they are solving. The bar will become higher, but I think that can also push people toward work they find more meaningful.

Software engineering is probably the clearest example of this happening today. AI can already write code, yet we still need software engineers. What is changing is what we need them for. An engineer who simply turns instructions into code is becoming less valuable than an engineer who understands architecture, knows what should be built and can direct AI toward building it. The engineer moves one level above the code itself. I think the same thing will happen everywhere else. An investor should spend more time understanding businesses and talking to founders and owners, not searching databases. A banker should spend more time advising a CEO, not formatting a pitch deck. As AI gets better, human work does not necessarily disappear. It moves toward the parts of the job that require judgment, creativity, relationships, and a real understanding of the problem.

We are still very early in this transition. Today, most people use AI by prompting it. We ask it to write an email, summarize a document, create a presentation or write code. This makes us more efficient, but the human is still responsible for moving the work forward. I think the next major shift comes when we stop giving AI tasks and start giving it responsibility. Instead of prompting an AI to “write an email to this person,” I should be able to tell it, “I want to get a meeting with this person by Friday.” It should understand the context, figure out what needs to happen, do the work, follow up when necessary, ask me when it needs my judgment and keep going until the work is done. At that point, we are no longer really interacting with AI like software. We are interacting with it in much the same way we interact with another employee.

Building this is much harder than simply putting an LLM inside a piece of software. These systems need to understand context, remember what has happened before, know when to act on their own, and know when a human needs to be involved. More importantly, they need to consistently produce results for customers. I think the companies that make this transition will need more than great technical talent. They will need a deep understanding of the actual workflows they are replacing and more importantly, the human interactions behind those workflows.

This is one of the reasons I’m so passionate about Equitle. When I worked in private equity helping source deals, much of my time was spent behind spreadsheets doing manual work: finding businesses, researching owners, finding their contact information, reaching out to them, following up and keeping track of everything. I had no innate interest in doing most of this and neither did almost every other analyst I spoke to. We did it because someone needed to do it. What I actually found interesting was understanding a business, learning why an owner had spent thirty years building it and figuring out whether there was an opportunity there. The spreadsheet was never the point. Finding a great business and building a relationship with its owner was.

That is what we are trying to change with Equitle. Instead of giving an investor another piece of software they have to operate, we are building agents that can actually carry the work forward. They can find companies, research owners, determine who might be interested in selling, reach out, follow up, and eventually bring the investor into the process when human judgment and relationships actually matter. To me, that is a small example of what the autonomous future looks like. The goal isn’t to make the analyst 20% faster at updating a spreadsheet. The goal is to make it unnecessary for a person to update that spreadsheet in the first place.

There is obviously an uncomfortable side to this future. People will lose jobs and I don’t think we should pretend that everyone who loses a job will immediately find something more meaningful to do. But I also don’t believe that humans will stop working as machines become more capable. Humans like keeping themselves occupied, competing, building things and solving bigger and bigger problems. During the Industrial Revolution, machines took over enormous amounts of physical labor, but that did not end human work. We found new problems to solve that would have been impossible for previous generations to even consider. I think AI will do something similar to intellectual work.

In fact, AI could make it easier for more people to pursue ideas they care about. Building a company today still requires engineers, salespeople, designers, accountants, lawyers, analysts, and many other people doing different types of work. If agents can eventually perform much of that work, a small group of people with an idea could accomplish what once required an entire company. Maybe eventually one person can. People who previously needed a corporation to give them the resources to work on something meaningful may increasingly be able to create those resources themselves. To me, this is where the idea of abundance becomes much more interesting. Abundance isn’t just making things cheaper. It is giving more people the ability to spend their time on problems they actually want to solve.

This is also why I think we need to redefine what productivity means in the age of AI. Today, when people talk about AI increasing productivity, they usually mean that one person can get more work done. An analyst can build a model faster. An engineer can write more code. A salesperson can send more emails. I think that is only the first stage. The bigger change happens when we stop asking how AI can help a human do mundane work faster and start asking why the human needs to be doing that work at all. If AI can take that work entirely off our hands, our attention can move somewhere else.

I don’t think the autonomous future is one where humans sit around because machines do everything for us. I think humans will continue doing what we have always done: finding harder problems. The problems we work on will change, and the amount that one person can accomplish will change dramatically, but our desire to build things and make progress will remain. To me, the most exciting promise of AI is therefore not that it will make us more efficient at the work we do today. It is that it can free us from much of that work altogether, leaving us with more time and energy to work on the problems that actually require us.

Chapter 1

16

When I was sixteen I worked at a private equity firm in Texas. It was the first time I saw the private markets up close. At the time I was buying stocks on Robinhood, so public markets were the only kind of investing I understood. I could open an app, buy a share in a few seconds and watch the price move the same afternoon.

Private markets worked nothing like that. Positions were illiquid, returns took years to settle, and a large part of the job was coordination: keeping limited partners informed, moving capital when it was needed and getting a number of people aligned before anything could happen. The investing itself took up far less of the week than I had assumed it would.

I didn’t leave that summer with a company in mind. What stayed with me was how much of the work was done by hand, and how little of that work had anything to do with judgment about a business.

Chapter 2

Sending donuts to private equity firms

The summer after my freshman year at UT I was working at another private equity fund, this one in real estate. While I was there I kept coming back to an idea: letting regular people invest in private equity funds. Not a single deal, the way most platforms offer it, but the fund itself. I wanted it to feel obvious to use, much closer to the Robinhood interface I had learned to invest on than to anything in institutional finance.

I spent that summer talking to as many lawyers as I could find, and eventually convinced a securities lawyer to come on as our in-house counsel. Once we had some clarity that the structure was possible at all, I wanted to hear it from the other side of the table. The firms had to want it too.

Getting an MD or a VP at a private equity firm to take a call from a college freshman is hard, so we had to be different. On top of cold emailing everyone we could reach, we drove around Houston dropping off boxes of donuts at firm offices with a note about what we were building.

It worked well enough to get us in the door, and the conversations taught us we had the problem backwards. The good funds were not struggling to raise. What came up again and again was the opposite end of the business: deploying the capital they already had. Private equity had become competitive enough that quality deal flow, not dry powder, was the real constraint.

Chapter 3

UChicago Booth Kellogg Conference

Once we knew that, we stopped defending the original idea and followed the problem the market was describing. My co-founder, Hazyk, had worked for two search funds, OxMar Partners and Next Gen Growth Partners under Charlie Ponitz, so we already knew that end of the market first hand.

A search fund is a vehicle that came out of Stanford: one entrepreneur raises money from a small group of investors to spend a year or two finding a single privately held company, buys it, and then runs it. There is no investment committee and no bench of associates. The searcher is the sourcing team, the analyst and the negotiator.

We knew large firms would be slow to become design partners, so we started with searchers instead. They are easy to get in touch with, and they are the most resource constrained buyers in the market. Nobody is building their target lists for them.

The annual ETA conference hosted by Chicago Booth and Northwestern Kellogg was coming up and we couldn’t afford to go. We convinced our school to fund the trip, $750 for three people, and convinced the program manager to give us tickets for free.

That conference is where we found our first beta users. We spent the months after it shadowing them, and the core of Equitle came out of watching how they actually spent their days. The front of the work broke into four steps: thesis development, choosing a market worth searching in; sourcing, building the list of target companies; enrichment, finding the owner’s contact information; and outreach, actually reaching those owners.

A reply only moves it along. Next comes qualification, working out from one short conversation whether a business is worth pursuing at all: what it really earns, how much of it walks out the door with the owner, and whether the owner is genuinely willing to sell. Most are not. From there a live one runs through an NDA before any financials change hands, an LOI to agree a price and go exclusive, a quality of earnings review to find out whether those numbers hold up, and diligence across customers, contracts and legal before anything can close. The first half of that is overhead. The second half is the real work, but it still runs on documents, checklists and follow-ups, and a searcher is doing all of it alone.

One of our advisors, Scott Derrick, an ex-searcher from Alidade Ventures, described the version of this problem that matters most. At one point during the search there was a live deal to work, which meant there was no time left to source. A search only lasts so long, and the pipeline goes quiet exactly when things are going well.

Building for that customer forced us to make the product genuinely hands off. Someone with a live deal and no team is not going to log into another dashboard and operate it. If it works for them, it will hold up when we move upstream to larger firms.

Chapter 4

Building the autonomous future

Sourcing is mundane work. Almost nobody went into investing to build lists, look up email addresses and keep a spreadsheet current. They do it because someone has to, and the interesting part of the job sits on the other side of it: understanding a business, learning why an owner spent thirty years building it, and working out whether there is an opportunity there. The spreadsheet was never the point.

Most software aimed at this only makes the manual work faster. Today people use AI by prompting it, which leaves the human responsible for moving the work forward. The shift I care about is the one where you stop handing an AI tasks and start handing it responsibility. Instead of asking it to write an email to an owner, I should be able to say I want a meeting with that owner by Friday, and expect it to understand the context, work out what has to happen, do it, follow up, come back when it needs my judgment, and keep going until the work is done.

That is what we are building at Equitle. Agents find companies, research owners, work out who might be open to selling, reach out, follow up, and bring the investor in at the point where relationships and judgment actually matter. The goal isn’t to make an analyst twenty percent faster at updating a spreadsheet. It is to make it unnecessary for a person to update it at all.

The hard part is not putting a language model inside software. It is context, memory, knowing when to act alone and when a person needs to be involved, and producing results consistently enough that a customer stops checking. That takes a deep understanding of the workflow being replaced and of the human interactions behind it, which is why we started by sitting next to the people doing the work.

I’ve written about where I think this goes more broadly in Autonomous Future.
